Vedanta begins with a radical proposition: beneath the changing forms of life
there is one unchanging reality. Its teachings move from that reality to the
nature of the world, then to the nature of the self and the paths by which that
nature can be realised.

These are my notes on five of its main tenets.

## 1. Brahman is the ultimate reality

Brahman is the one without a second: existence, consciousness and bliss
absolute. It is beyond name, form and qualities; without beginning or end;
unchanging and beyond space, time and causation.

This vast, infinite Brahman manifests as the universe and as individual beings
through the power of **maya**. The many forms we meet do not stand apart from
the one reality. They are its appearance.

## 2. The universe is in the mind

The universe appears to be continuously changing. We perceive it through
space, time and causation: space begins with the body, time begins when thought
begins, and causation begins when the unlimited appears limited.

In deep sleep, the beautiful universe disappears from our awareness. In the
waking state, it appears again. The same disappearance is described in
samadhi, when ordinary mental distinctions fall away. Vedanta therefore asks
us to examine the mind through which a world appears, rather than treating our
usual perception of that world as the final truth.

## 3. Human beings are divine

Our real nature is **Atman**: infinite, eternal, pure, luminous, ever free and
blissful. Atman is identical with Brahman.

From this perspective, human beings are not sinners by nature. We make
mistakes because of ignorance—because we do not recognise what we are. Joy is
found in freedom, and Vedanta declares freedom to be inherent in every being.
The goal of human life is to realise God; the purpose of religion is to realise
the divinity already within.

## 4. The four yogas manifest that divinity

Vedanta offers four paths suited to different temperaments. They are not
competing destinations, but ways of approaching the same truth:

- **Karma yoga** — the path of unselfish action
- **Jnana yoga** — the path of knowledge
- **Bhakti yoga** — the path of devotion
- **Raja yoga** — the path of meditation

Each path turns an ordinary human capacity—action, reason, love or
attention—toward freedom. A life may emphasise one, but the paths can support
and deepen one another.

## 5. Truth is one and universal

If reality is one, no sincere path has a monopoly on it. Names, forms,
practices and doctrines differ, but truth itself is universal. Vedanta's unity
does not ask us to erase those differences. It asks us to see what they point
toward.

The movement of these teachings is therefore both vast and intimate: from the
absolute nature of Brahman to the divinity within every person, and from that
recognition to a life of action, knowledge, devotion and meditation.
