## What it says

Every task competes for attention on two independent axes: how urgent it is and
how important it is. Urgency is about time pressure. Importance is about
consequence. The matrix crosses the two and produces four quadrants, each with
its own verb.

Urgent and important work gets done now. Important but not urgent work gets a
scheduled place in the calendar — this is the quadrant where long-term work
actually lives, and the one that noise crowds out first. Urgent but unimportant
work gets delegated or reduced to the smallest honest version. Work that is
neither urgent nor important gets deleted without ceremony.

The matrix is useful because urgency is loud and importance is quiet. Left
alone, a day fills itself with whatever shouts. Sorting by two axes instead of
one is a small move that changes what a week adds up to.

Consider a hypothetical week in software. An outage is urgent and important:
users are affected now. Improving the recovery process is important but can
wait today, so it needs protected time before the next outage. An interruption
may feel urgent because somebody is waiting, yet belong with a different
person. Checking a dashboard repeatedly without a decision to make may be
neither. The point is not that these examples always belong in these boxes.
It is that their deadlines and consequences answer different questions.

Importance needs a reference point. Important to whom, and toward what? A task
can be unimportant to one project but essential to a promise made to somebody
else. Naming the outcome first keeps the matrix from becoming an excuse to
ignore inconvenient responsibilities. Delegation also requires a real owner
with the capacity and context to act; it does not mean forwarding an obligation
and forgetting it.

Treat the boxes as a review, not a permanent label. A scheduled task can become
urgent. New information can make an apparently minor issue consequential.
The useful result is a changed calendar or a clear next action, not a perfectly
sorted list. In the diagram, pick a sample task and place it where it belongs
under the circumstances you have in mind. Then change those circumstances and
try again. The judgement is the exercise; the square is only a way to see it.

## Where it comes from

The underlying distinction is attributed to Dwight D. Eisenhower, who is widely
quoted as separating the urgent from the important. Stephen Covey popularised
the four-quadrant form in _The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People_ as the
time-management matrix. FranklinCovey's
[Habit 3: Put First Things First](https://www.franklincovey.com/courses/the-7-habits/habit-3/)
explains the urgency–importance distinction and protecting important,
non-urgent work. The software examples above are illustrations, not claims
about Divyum's personal practice.
