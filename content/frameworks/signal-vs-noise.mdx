## What it says

Much of what competes for attention can be noise relative to a particular
decision: plausible, urgent-sounding, but not useful to the question at hand.
Signal is the information that changes that decision. The distinction is not
an intrinsic property of a message. The same detail may be irrelevant to one
question and decisive for another.

The working idea here is subtraction. Attention cannot expand to meet every
possible demand, so choosing a direction also means choosing what not to
pursue. That includes attractive alternatives, not just obviously bad ones.
Focus has a cost: something worthwhile remains undone so that something else
can receive enough sustained attention to become good.

That is what the visual above argues by demonstration: stare at the full field
and there is nothing to see. Filter it, and a shape was there the whole time.

A practical way to explore the principle is to name one question before
collecting more information. Suppose a team wants to understand why a form is
being abandoned. A change in completion rate, a reproducible error, or a
reader's account of a confusing field might affect the next decision. A stream
of unrelated feature requests might not. Narrowing the question gives each
incoming detail a job to do.

But filtering is not the same as dismissing disagreement. A rule that removes
every awkward observation can make a wrong idea feel wonderfully clear.
Useful filters need a way back out: revisit the question, inspect a sample of
what was excluded, and look deliberately for evidence that would change the
decision. Simplicity is valuable only when it preserves what matters.

The diagram is an analogy, not a model of how all knowledge works. Its signal
is deliberately fixed in advance. In real work, the shape may not be known,
and some apparent noise may reveal a better question. Use the interaction to
notice the relief of a less crowded field, then remember the judgement hidden
behind the filter. Removing marks is easy. Choosing what deserves to remain
is the part that requires thought.

## Where it comes from

This is an interpretation of Steve Jobs's focus philosophy, not a formal
framework published under this name. In his
[1997 speech to Apple employees](https://book.stevejobsarchive.com/#speech-apple-1997),
preserved in the Steve Jobs Archive's _Make Something Wonderful_, he describes
cutting the product roadmap and concentrating on fewer products. The filtering
diagram and examples here illustrate that principle; they are not Jobs's own
diagram or an exact quotation.
