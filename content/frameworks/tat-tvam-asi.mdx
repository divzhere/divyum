## What it says

This is a student's summary of Advaita Vedanta, following the study notes
behind this page and developed more fully in [The Main Tenets of
Vedanta](/essays/main-tenets-of-vedanta). It presents a philosophical account
of self and reality, not a scientific finding or a claim that every school of
Indian philosophy agrees. The central question is what remains when changing
names, forms, and experiences are not mistaken for the whole of who we are.

Advaita Vedanta begins with Brahman: the ultimate reality, the one without a
second. It is existence, consciousness, bliss — absolute; beyond name, form and
qualities; without beginning or end. Unchanging truth beyond space, time and
causation. This vast, infinite Brahman manifests as the self, as the universe,
and as individual beings through the power of maya.

The universe, in this view, is apparent and continuously changing. We perceive
it through space. Space starts with the body. Time starts when one starts
thinking. Causation begins when one becomes limited. The universe disappears in
deep sleep or in samadhi and reappears in the waking state — this world is in
the mind.

In this account, human beings are divine. The real nature is the atma — infinite, eternal, pure,
luminous, ever-free, blissful, identical with Brahman. People are not sinners;
they make mistakes because of ignorance. Joy is only in freedom, which Vedanta
declares to be the inherent nature of all beings. The goal of human life is to
realize this — the purpose of religion is to realize the divinity within.

Vedanta names four yogas as paths to manifest it: karma yoga, the path of
unselfish action; jnana yoga, the path of knowledge; bhakti yoga, the path of
devotion; and raja yoga, the path of meditation. Yoga means union of the
individual soul with the cosmic soul. Truth is one and universal — the paths
differ, the centre does not.

The visual holds the whole argument in one form: seen from one layer it reads
as many; seen from the other it was always one.

## How I use it

I use this framework as a map for study rather than a conclusion to memorise.
It brings the central questions back into view: what changes, what observes the
change, and what remains when name and form are set aside? The four yogas then
turn those questions toward daily life through action, knowledge, devotion and
meditation.

The framework is also a reminder not to confuse a description with direct
realisation. Reading can give the ideas precision; practice asks whether they
change how I understand freedom, other people and myself.

## Where it comes from

Tat tvam asi — "that thou art" — is one of the mahavakyas, the great sayings of
the Upanishads, taught in the Chandogya Upanishad and central to Adi
Shankaracharya's Advaita Vedanta. The summary above follows Divyum's own study
notes in the Vedanta tradition of Swami Vivekananda; it is a student's account,
not a claim of mastery.

For a primary text in that interpretive tradition, see Vivekananda's
[Practical Vedanta, Part III](https://www.ramakrishnavivekananda.info/vivekananda/volume_2/practical_vedanta_and_other_lectures/practical_vedanta_part_iii.htm).
It discusses the Chandogya teaching to Shvetaketu and uses the examples of
honey and rivers to examine unity. The visual here is an aid to reading these
ideas, not evidence for them.
